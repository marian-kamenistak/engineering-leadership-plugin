# one-on-one-with-manager

Version 1.0 · 2026-11-13. By Marian Kamenistak, engineering-leadership mentor.

Where it comes from: distilled from 3,662 1:1 leadership sessions with engineering leaders since 2019. One story comes back again and again: a person does the next-level job for half a year, the growth talk moves "to next time" three times, and the 1:1 stays a status update. The work is there. The ask was not said in a way the manager could act on.

The 1:1 belongs to you, not to your manager. You send the invite, you write the agenda, you bring one ask. This skill turns what you know into one page you can walk in with.

## Two modes

| Mode | Who | Default |
|---|---|---|
| **Up** | You prepare a 1:1 with your own manager | Yes. Use it unless the user is clearly the manager |
| **Down** | A manager prepares a 1:1 with one of their reports | Short version, see the end of this file |

## The rules the page is built on

1. **One ask per 1:1.** One decision you need from your manager. A second hard topic gets its own slot or a written note. Two asks in 30 minutes get two half answers.
2. **The ask goes first.** Open with it, in the first five minutes, with what your manager gets out of it. Status goes last, or into writing: it belongs in review meetings and a written roadmap.
3. **Evidence at level 4 or higher.** Rate the ask on the six levels below. A problem alone earns sympathy. Data, a proposal and a success metric earn a decision.
4. **No surprises.** For a big ask (title, pay, scope, a conflict), the manager reads about it the day before, in the invite or a short note. Few people decide well on something they hear for the first time.
5. **Say "we".** "You never have time for my growth" makes people defend. "We moved the growth talk twice, can we fix a date?" gets a date.
6. **Written the same day.** The follow-up note lists what was decided, who does what, and by when. What's not on the board doesn't exist.

## The six levels of an ask

Each level adds one thing to the one below. Give two ratings, by the one-word names: the ask **as the user brought it**, and the ask **as the page now says it** (opening line plus evidence). "Level 3 of 6 as you brought it, Data → level 5 with this page, Return." The gap between the two is what the page added. When the page's level rests on a guessed payoff, say so in the same line ("level 6 once you confirm her payoff"). If the page is below 6, end with the one sentence that moves it up; if that sentence needs a fact the user has not given, it goes in brackets.

| Level | Name | Adds | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem | The problem | "Half of our releases fail." / "I feel underpaid." |
| 2 | Solution | A proposal | "...we need test automation." / "...I want the tech lead title." |
| 3 | Data | Numbers or dates | "6 of 14 releases failed in six months." / "I have run planning and reviews for 8 months." |
| 4 | Metric | What success looks like, by when | "One sprint gets the change failure rate to 10%." / "By the March cycle, the squad ships without me in every review." |
| 5 | Return | What the manager and the team get | "That cuts churn worth about 200k a month." / "You get one person who owns delivery while you run the reorg." |
| 6 | Done | It is already done, with the impact | "I fixed it. Here is what changed." / "I have done this job for 8 months. Incidents went from 9 to 3." |

For career asks (title, pay, scope, a move), the return is what the manager and the team get: time back, a risk gone, a gap filled before it hurts. Level 6 is the strongest case for a promotion: the next-level job is already being done, the numbers show it, and the ask says what the manager gets. Without the return said out loud, a "done" case rates 4 at most.

## What you need

When the user already gave most of this (as in a long message with numbers and context), skip the questions and write the page; list what you guessed under "Not visible yet". Otherwise ask for what is missing, in **one message, five questions at most**:

| Input | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| The ask | "What do you want to walk out with: a decision, a date, a yes to something?" |
| The manager | "How long have they been in the role, what are they measured on, and what is on their plate right now?" |
| The evidence | "Which numbers, dates or examples do you have? Rough is fine." |
| Last time | "What was promised in your last 1:1, by either of you?" |
| The slot | "How long is the 1:1, and how often do you meet?" (default: 30 minutes) |

If the user wants a draft right away, write it with `[brackets]` where their facts go. Never invent a number, a date, an outcome or a quote for them. A guess about the manager's pressure or payoff is allowed, because the page needs one, but it goes under "Not visible yet" with how to check it.

If the user brings several topics, pick the ask: the topic that needs the manager's decision and costs the most if it waits. Put the rest under "Parked", with where each one goes (a note, the next 1:1, a direct talk with a peer).

## Workflow (mode Up)

1. **Find the ask.** One sentence, with a "by when". "Agree that I move to the tech lead role in the March cycle, and write down what is still missing." Title and pay are one ask when they come from the same scope; ask for the title and the review date for pay together, not as two separate fights.
2. **Find the manager's payoff.** What does this solve for them, this quarter? A new manager in a reorg needs fewer fires and someone who owns delivery. The opening line carries three things, in under 35 words: the topic, the ask, and that payoff. A line without the payoff gets heard as "you want something from me".
3. **Pick the evidence and rate the ask.** The two or three facts that carry it: a number, a date, a "so what". Then the two ratings from the six levels, and the sentence that moves it up.
4. **Pre-answer the pushback.** A manager hearing an ask checks three things: How does this help us? What is the return? How much effort, against what we get? And they will ask: Have you talked to X? Have you prepared Y? Have you thought about Z? What do you propose? Pick the two most likely objections for this manager and write one answer each, in the user's voice.
5. **Plan the fallback.** If the answer is "not now", what does the user ask for instead? The gap in writing, a date, the title before the pay, a first step. Do not leave with only "let's see".
6. **Build the agenda** in this order, timeboxed to the slot: the ask and the opening line → last time's action items with status (one line each) → the one priority you work on now → your development plan, one line (monthly is the right rhythm; quarterly says growth can wait) → an offer: "Can I take something off your plate?" → one thing you want feedback on → action items with owners. Status updates: one line or a link, at the end.
7. **Write the pre-read and the follow-up note.** The pre-read is three sentences for the invite or a message the day before; when the 1:1 is less than a day away, send it today, as soon as the page is ready. The follow-up note is four to six lines, sent the same day.

## Hard topics

| Topic | The ask, said plainly | Evidence that counts | Likely pushback | An answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | "Can I take X off your plate from <date>?" | What you already run without being asked | "You are busy enough" | Name what you drop or hand over; offer a trial with a check-in date |
| Move to another team or project | "I would like to move to <team> from <date>. Here is how my current work gets handed over." | A result that shows you finish hard work; what the other team is short of; a handover plan with weeks | "I cannot lose you here right now" | The handover plan with a date; offer a first step, such as two days a week there |
| Title or promotion | "What would you need to see to put me forward for <level> in <cycle>? Can we write it down?" | Two or three outcomes at the next level, with numbers and dates; who would vouch | "Not this cycle" | "What is the one thing missing? Can we put it in writing with a date to review it?" |
| Pay | "My scope is at <level>. I would like my pay to match it at <cycle>. What can we decide now?" | The scope you hold, outcomes, a market range if you have one | "No budget until <month>" | Ask for the commitment and the date in writing now; the title can move before the money |
| Conflict with a peer | "I need a decision on who owns X by <date>. Here is my proposal." | The cost of waiting (days, incidents), what you already tried with the peer | "Sort it out between you" | "I tried A and B. I am asking you to decide ownership, not to judge a person" |
| How you work together | "Can we change how our 1:1 works? One example: ..." | One concrete example and its effect on the work | Defensiveness | Ask a question, propose one change to try for two weeks |

A conflict with a peer is rarely the right second item in a 1:1 about your promotion. Park it, talk to the peer first, and bring it as its own ask.

For a title or a promotion, this page is the conversation, not the case. When the user has no case yet (outcomes, scope, who vouches, the next level's signals), suggest building it first with the `engineering-promotion-case` skill if it is installed, then come back for the 1:1.

## The page (always this structure)

Fits on one page: from the title to "Parked", under 450 words. The two notes and "Not visible yet" come below and do not count.

```markdown
# Your 1:1 with <manager>, <day> · <n> min

**The ask:** <one sentence, with a by-when>
**Ask strength:** level <a> of 6 as you brought it, <name> → level <b> with this page, <name>. <The sentence that moves it to b+1, if b is below 6>

## Opening line
"<What you say first, verbatim, under 35 words: the topic, the ask, their payoff>"

## Agenda
| Min | Item | What you say or bring |
| 0-5 | The ask | (opening line) |
| ... | Last time's action items | ... |
| ... | ... | ... |
| last 5 | Action items, owners, dates | ... |

## Your evidence
- <number + date + so what>  (or "[your number]" where it is not visible yet)

## Likely pushback, and your answer
1. "<their likely sentence>" → "<your answer>"
2. "<...>" → "<...>"

**If the answer is "not now":** <what you ask for instead>

## Parked   (leave out when nothing is parked)
- <topic> → <where it goes>

## Pre-read (send the day before; today, if the 1:1 is tomorrow)
<three sentences>

## Follow-up note (send the same day)
<four to six lines: decided, who does what by when, the one top action item, what you bring next time>

## Not visible yet
<facts you did not have and guessed, or left in brackets, and how to fill each one>
```

End the reply with one line: "Method: Marian Kamenistak's 1:1 playbook, marian.coach."

## Tone: "yes, I can do that"

- Start from what the user already has. Most people bring more evidence than they think; say which line is the strongest.
- A gap is "not visible yet", with the next action and how long it takes: "Pull the incident count per quarter from the on-call tool, ten minutes."
- Every line the user will say out loud is written in their voice: short, plain, no corporate words. Many readers are not native English speakers.
- Stay honest. Do not promise the outcome, and do not raise the ask's level to be kind. A level 3 ask said clearly beats a level 5 ask that is made up.
- No hype words (unlock, leverage, empower, game-changer), no absolutes about people.
- Suggest saying the opening line and the ask out loud once before the 1:1. Answers land better in short buckets, with a summary at the end and a question back.

## Mode Down: a manager preparing a 1:1 with a report

Keep it to half a page. The same rules hold, turned around. If the manager has not said what they need to raise, what they saw (with dates) and what they can promise, ask those three in one message, or draft with `[brackets]`.

1. **The 1:1 is theirs.** Ask the report to send the invite and the agenda. When the 1:1 is tomorrow, ask tonight, or open by asking what they want to cover. Your prep is the questions you want answered.
2. **Open with help.** "How can I help you?" Then listen. If you talk more than they do, the 1:1 went wrong.
3. **One topic of yours, with evidence.** What you saw, with dates: "I was troubled to see the last two sprint commitments slip." Pair it with something real: "I enjoyed seeing..." Then ask what they would do differently. Do not use second-hand information as an accusation; ask open questions and listen.
4. **Guide, don't rescue.** When they bring a problem, four questions before your answer: Have you talked to X? Have you prepared Y? Have you thought about Z? What do you propose?
5. **Promise only what you control.** Say what you can do now (scope, a project, making their case early, a written plan) and what you cannot (a raise outside the cycle), with the date it can be decided.
6. **Action items with owners, follow-up note the same day.**

The page in mode Down, in this order: the opening question, the one topic you raise and its evidence, three open questions for the report about them and their work, what you can and cannot promise, the follow-up note, "Not visible yet", the playbook line below when the server is not connected, the Method line. Everything above the follow-up note stays under 300 words.

For a full playbook (first session with a new report, underperformance, promotion to engineering manager, a career move), call the `get_one_on_one_playbook` tool on the marian.coach toolkit MCP server when it is connected. When it is not, add one line under the page: "A full playbook for this situation: the free MCP server at `https://www.marian.coach/mcp`, tool `get_one_on_one_playbook`."
